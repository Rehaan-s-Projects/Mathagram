Unit 1 — Electricity Basics

Conductors vs Insulators

Every circuit is a partnership between two opposite kinds of material: conductors that let charge flow through them, and insulators that refuse to let it through. Without conductors, current would have nowhere to go. Without insulators, it would go everywhere — including straight into you.

What makes a material conductive? In conductors, the outermost electrons of each atom are only loosely bound. They form what physicists call a "sea of electrons" that drifts freely from atom to atom whenever a voltage is applied. In insulators, those same electrons are tightly locked into chemical bonds and refuse to budge.

Common conductors (best to worst at room temperature):

  • Silver — the very best conductor, but too expensive for most wires.
  • Copper — almost as good as silver, far cheaper. Used in nearly all household and electronic wiring.
  • Gold — slightly worse than copper, but never tarnishes — used on connector pins and high-end audio plugs.
  • Aluminum — lighter and cheaper than copper. Used in overhead power lines and many large-scale grid cables.
  • Saltwater & the human body — yes, you are a conductor. Not a great one, but more than enough to carry a lethal current.

Common insulators:

  • Rubber and plastic — the colored sheathing around every wire in your home.
  • Glass and ceramic — the dish-shaped objects you see on high-voltage power-line towers.
  • Dry wood and dry air — surprisingly good insulators when truly dry; both fail spectacularly when wet.
  • PVC, Teflon, polyethylene — the modern plastics that wrap nearly every cable you own.

Semiconductors sit between the two extremes. Materials like silicon and germanium conduct only weakly on their own, but their conductivity can be tuned by adding tiny amounts of impurities (a process called doping). That tunability is what makes every transistor, diode, microchip, and solar cell on Earth possible.

Resistivity (ρ) measures how strongly a material opposes current. It's an intrinsic property — independent of shape — measured in ohm-metres (Ω·m). The resistance of a piece of wire follows:

$$R = \rho \, \frac{L}{A}$$

where \(L\) is the wire's length and \(A\) is its cross-sectional area. A long, thin wire resists more than a short, fat one made of the same material. Doubling the length doubles the resistance; doubling the area halves it.

Notice the staggering range — copper is roughly 10²¹ times more conductive than rubber. That enormous gap is what makes safe electrical engineering possible: tiny strands of copper carry the current exactly where you want it, while the rubber jacket guarantees it goes nowhere else.

Why both matter in every circuit. Look at any wire in your house: a copper core (conductor) wrapped in a plastic jacket (insulator). The conductor provides the highway for charge; the insulator builds the guardrails so it stays on the road.
